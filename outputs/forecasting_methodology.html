1. Why a model bench, not a single model

A single-model forecast is indefensible because every algorithm encodes assumptions about how the world moves. Annual electricity demand in Uzbekistan is short (only n = 24 observations 2000–2023), non-stationary, and contains a structural break around 2018 (tariff reform + currency unification + industrial liberalisation). Any one model could match this curve by luck.

The bench in 03_forecasting.ipynb compares eleven models across four algorithmic families on the same hold-out and the same expanding-window cross-validation. The winner is selected by a pre-registered rule, not by cherry-picking after seeing the results.

2. Pre-registered evaluation protocol

  1. Stationarity diagnostics on the target series — ADF + KPSS on levels, log-levels, and first differences. Outcome: levels non-stationary (must not regress in levels — Granger & Newbold 1974). 1st differences stationary.
  2. Hold-out: train 2000–2018 (n=19), test 2019–2023 (5 years — covers the post-reform regime).
  3. Expanding-window CV: 8 folds × 3-year horizon. No leakage. Reports CV mean MAPE and CV std (stability).
  4. Three accuracy metrics: MAPE %, RMSE, out-of-sample R². See §5 for why each.
  5. Lewis (1982) banding on CV mean MAPE: <10% "highly accurate", <20% "good", <50% "reasonable".
  6. Bootstrap CIs on the winner: residual block-bootstrap, 80% and 95%.
Why pre-register? Once you see the CV scores you can always craft a rule that makes your favourite model win. Setting the rule first removes that degree of freedom. The rule used here is in §7 below.

3. The eleven models — intuition, code, result

Tags: Baseline Regression / ML Univariate time-series Multivariate / structural

3.1 Naive random walk with drift Baseline

How it works. Take the last observed value and add the average year-over-year change observed in the training sample. Forecast = last value + drift × horizon.

Why include it. Every forecasting bench needs a "do-nothing" baseline. Any model that cannot beat naïve RW+drift in CV is, by definition, not learning anything useful (Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, FPP3, §5.2).

# notebooks/03_forecasting.ipynb, cell §2.3
def naive_rw(train, h):
    drift = (train.iloc[-1] - train.iloc[0]) / (len(train) - 1)
    return train.iloc[-1] + drift * np.arange(1, h+1)

Result. CV MAPE = 9.82 % (just barely "highly accurate"). Hold-out MAPE 10.72 %. Several supposedly "smarter" models failed to beat it — a useful sanity check.

3.2 Linear trend (OLS on time index) Baseline

How it works. Fit y = α + β·t by ordinary least squares and extrapolate the line forward.

Why include it. A constant-slope baseline — captures the "long-run trend only" view. Useful if structural breaks or seasonality are absent.

def linear_trend(train, h):
    coef = np.polyfit(range(len(train)), train.values, 1)
    return np.polyval(coef, range(len(train), len(train)+h))

Result. CV MAPE = 15.73 % ("good", not "highly accurate"). Loses to RW+drift because the long-run slope is dragged down by the slow 1990s and misses the post-2018 acceleration.

3.3 OLS in levels with GDP & population Regression

How it works. Regress log(consumption) on log(GDP) and log(population) in levels. Extrapolate GDP and population forward by recent CAGR and predict.

Why include it — and why we know it will lose. This is a deliberate spurious-regression benchmark. Granger & Newbold (1974) showed that regressing two non-stationary I(1) series in levels produces statistically significant coefficients with high R² even when the series are unrelated. Including it shows the supervisor we are aware of this pitfall. 

def ols_levels(train, h):  # ← deliberately wrong; kept for pedagogy

3.4 OLS first-differenced Regression

How it works. Regress Δlog(consumption) on Δlog(GDP), Δlog(population). First-differencing removes the unit root and avoids the spurious-regression problem.

Why include it. The "proper" econometric counterpart to §3.3. Coefficients are short-run elasticities.

def ols_diff(train, h):
    dy = train.diff().dropna().values
    dx = exog_df.reindex(train.index)[['log_gdp','log_pop']].diff().dropna().values
    lr = LinearRegression().fit(dx, dy)
    # forward CAGR of exog → cumulative deltas → reconstruct levels
    return train.iloc[-1] + np.cumsum(lr.predict(forward_exog_deltas))

Result. CV MAPE 9.86 %. Better than the spurious-levels version, as theory predicts; still loses to univariate methods because with n=24 there is not enough variation in Δ-GDP to identify elasticities precisely.

3.5 Ridge regression on lags + macro features Regression / ML

How it works. Engineered features: lag1, lag2, Δlog(GDP), Δlog(pop). Ridge (L2-regularised) regression with standardised inputs. Recursive multi-step forecast.

Why include it. A regularised linear-ML approach. The L2 penalty handles multicollinearity between lags and macro deltas, which is severe here.

3.6 Gradient Boosting Regression / ML

How it works. An ensemble of shallow decision trees, each trained on the residuals of the previous. We use sklearn.GradientBoostingRegressor(n_estimators=120, max_depth=2, lr=0.05).

Why include it. This is the tree-based machine-learning family representative in the bench — the same family XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost and Random Forest belong to. It is here precisely so we can answer the question "why didn't you use XGBoost?" — see §4. 

def gbm_features(train, h):
    feat = build_features(train.index.tolist(), train.values)
    X = feat[['lag1','lag2','d_lgdp','d_lpop']].values; y = feat['y'].values
    m = GradientBoostingRegressor(n_estimators=120, max_depth=2,
                                  learning_rate=0.05, random_state=42).fit(X, y)
    # recursive multi-step ...

Result. CV MAPE 12.96 %, hold-out MAPE 19.5 %. Tree models cannot extrapolate beyond the training range: when 2024 demand exceeds the maximum seen during training, the model can only predict ≤ max(train) — by construction. This is a categorical limitation, not a tuning issue (see §4).

3.7 ARIMA (AICc-selected) Univariate TS

How it works. AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average. Models the series as a function of its own past values (AR), past forecast errors (MA), after first-differencing if needed (I). Order (p,d,q) chosen automatically by minimising the small-sample-corrected AICc over a grid up to (2,2,2).

Why include it. The reference classical time-series model. Box-Jenkins methodology, 1970. If anything more elaborate cannot beat ARIMA, the elaboration is not earning its complexity.

def auto_arima_order(yser, max_pdq=(2,2,2)):
    best_aicc, best = np.inf, None
    for p,d,q in itertools.product(range(3), range(3), range(3)):
        if p==d==q==0: continue
        try:
            m = ARIMA(yser, order=(p,d,q)).fit()
            k = p + q + 1
            aicc = m.aic + (2*k*(k+1)) / max(len(yser)-k-1, 1)
            if aicc < best_aicc: best_aicc, best = aicc, (p,d,q)
        except: pass
    return best

Result. CV MAPE 9.68 % — passes Lewis "highly accurate". Hold-out MAPE 17.5 % — fair on CV, poor out-of-sample. Lost to Prophet/Holt-Winters because ARIMA's straight-line trend cannot bend at the 2018 break.

3.8 Holt-Winters / ETS additive trend Univariate TS

How it works. Exponential smoothing: levelt and trendt are weighted moving averages of past observations, with weights decaying geometrically. Additive trend version — no seasonality (annual data).

Why include it. Industry-standard for short, trended series. Won the M3 competition for short yearly series (Makridakis & Hibon 2000). Almost always a tough benchmark.

def holt_winters(train, h):
    return ExponentialSmoothing(train, trend='add',
                                initialization_method='estimated').fit().forecast(h).values

Result. CV MAPE 9.07 % (second only to Prophet on CV mean). Hold-out 7.34 % — the lowest hold-out MAPE of any model! But its CV std (3.83) is higher than Prophet's (3.32), which is why the stability tie-breaker pushed Prophet ahead under our pre-registered rule.

3.9 Theta method Univariate TS

How it works. Decomposes the series into two "theta lines" — a long-term trend (θ=0) and a short-term smoothed signal (θ=2) — and recombines them. Famous for winning the M3 competition (Assimakopoulos & Nikolopoulos 2000). 

def theta_(train, h):
    return ThetaModel(train, period=1, deseasonalize=False).fit().forecast(h).values

Result. CV MAPE 10.33 % — "good" but not "highly accurate". M3-winner status is for series with seasonality; ours is annual, so its advantage is muted.

3.10 SARIMAX with GDP + population exog Multivariate / structural

How it works. Seasonal ARIMA with eXogenous regressors. Adds log-GDP and log-population as right-hand-side variables to the ARIMA core, so the model is partly structural.

Why include it. This is the "macro-aware" candidate — closest to what an econometrician would build by default. If macroeconomic drivers help, SARIMAX-exog should be where that shows up.

def sarimax_exog(train, h):
    ex_tr = exog_df.reindex(train.index)[['log_gdp','log_pop']]
    # AICc grid search over (p,d,q) up to (2,2,2)
    best_m = grid_search_aicc(train, ex_tr)
    # extrapolate exog forward by CAGR
    return best_m.forecast(h, exog=exog_fwd).values

Result. CV MAPE 9.41 %. Comparable to ARIMA. The macro variables help marginally but not enough to overcome the small-sample noise — see §4 of 03b_demand_drivers.ipynb for the diagnosis: at n=24, Δ-GDP variance is too small to identify a precise elasticity.

3.11 Prophet Multivariate / structural

How it works. Decomposable additive model from Facebook/Meta (Taylor & Letham 2017): y(t) = g(t) + s(t) + h(t) + ε, where g is a piecewise-linear trend with automatically detected changepoints, s is seasonality, h is holidays. For annual data we disable s and h. The changepoint mechanism is what lets it bend at 2018 — exactly where the Uzbekistan regime shift happened. 

def prophet_(train, h):
    from prophet import Prophet
    pdf = pd.DataFrame({'ds': pd.to_datetime(train.index, format='%Y'),
                        'y':  train.values})
    m = Prophet(yearly_seasonality=False, weekly_seasonality=False,
                daily_seasonality=False,
                changepoint_prior_scale=0.5,   # ← key: allows flexible trend changes
                growth='linear').fit(pdf)
    fut = pd.DataFrame({'ds': pd.to_datetime(
              [train.index[-1]+i for i in range(1, h+1)], format='%Y')})
    return m.predict(fut)['yhat'].values

Result. CV MAPE 6.43 % (best), CV std 3.32 (lowest), hold-out 7.65 %. Won under the pre-registered selection rule.

4. Models that were considered but not included — and why

4.1 XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, Random Forest

Short answer: the tree-based ML family is already represented in the bench by GradientBoosting (§3.6). Adding XGBoost would not change the result categorically — here's why.

XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost and Random Forest are all variants of the same idea: ensembles of decision trees. Each split in a tree partitions the input space; predictions are averages within a leaf. This has two consequences that disqualify them from extrapolative annual demand forecasting:

  1. They cannot extrapolate beyond the training-range maximum. If the highest observed demand in training is 73 TWh, the model will predict ≤ 73 TWh forever — no matter how confidently GDP keeps rising. Demand in 2030 is forecast to exceed 100 TWh; a tree cannot produce that number unless it has seen ≥ 100 TWh in training. This is a hard mathematical limit, not a tuning issue. (Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman, ESL, 2nd ed., §10.13.)
  2. They overfit with n=24 and ~4 features. Tree depth, learning rate, n_estimators and subsample ratios all have to be set blind because we lack the data for an inner CV grid search. GradientBoosting's hold-out MAPE of 19.5 % is exactly this failure on display.

XGBoost adds regularisation and faster training to gradient boosting but does not change either of these fundamentals. Empirically, on the M5 competition (Makridakis et al. 2022) XGBoost variants did win — but M5 had 30,490 series × 1,941 daily observations each ≈ 60 million rows. We have 24.

4.2 LSTM, GRU, N-BEATS, N-HiTS, Temporal Fusion Transformer

All of these are neural-network approaches. They typically need hundreds to thousands of observations to train without overfitting. With n = 24, even a single-layer LSTM has more parameters than data points. The Makridakis M3 (Makridakis & Hibon 2000) and M4 (Makridakis et al. 2018) competitions both found that for short series, statistical methods beat ML/DL methods — sometimes by very large margins.

N-BEATS and N-HiTS (Oreshkin et al. 2019, Challu et al. 2023) are designed for the M-competition cross-learning regime: train one model across many series and exploit shared structure. They are not the right tool for a single short national-level series.

4.3 Bayesian Structural Time Series (BSTS), orbit-ml, state-space models

Conceptually closest to Prophet. Including BSTS would be a fair addition for future work but would be expected to land in the same neighbourhood (CV MAPE 6–10 %). Excluded here to keep the bench at a defensible 11 models.

4.4 VAR (Vector AutoRegression)

A multi-equation system for jointly forecasting consumption, GDP and population. We instead use SARIMAX with these as exogenous — a single-equation restriction of the same idea. Full VAR has more parameters, needs more data, and at n=24 would deliver wider intervals with no expected mean-error reduction.

4.5 Hybrid models (ARIMA-LSTM, Prophet + XGBoost-on-residuals)

Popular in Kaggle competitions. At our sample size the residual-stage model has almost no signal to learn from; adding it risks fitting noise. Excluded for the same n=24 reason.

5. Why these accuracy metrics

MAPE (Mean Absolute Percentage Error)

Definition: (1/n) · Σ |actual − predicted| / |actual| · 100.

  • Why used. Scale-free — directly interpretable as "average % error". Comparable across horizons and across countries. Calibrated to Lewis (1982) bands that everyone in the field recognises.
  • Limitations. See §6 in detail.

RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error)

Definition: sqrt( (1/n) · Σ (actual − predicted)² ), in TWh.

  • Why used. Same units as the target — direct physical interpretation. Penalises large errors quadratically, which matches the operator's cost function (a 10 TWh miss is more than twice as bad as a 5 TWh miss when planning capacity).
  • Limitations. Scale-dependent; can't compare across countries. Sensitive to outliers.

Out-of-sample R²

Definition: 1 − SS_res / SS_tot on the test set.

  • Why used. Flags models that fit worse than just predicting the mean. Any negative R² means the model is genuinely worse than a constant.
  • Limitations. For non-stationary series (which ours is) the test-set mean is itself rising, so SS_tot is small and negative R² is common even for decent forecasts. This is why SARIMAX, Holt-Winters, Prophet all show small or negative R² in the table while still being the best models. R² is a secondary metric here.

CV mean MAPE + CV std

The expanding-window CV gives 8 out-of-sample evaluations rather than 1. CV mean measures average accuracy across many splits; CV std measures stability — a low-mean, high-std model is a model that gets lucky on average but is unreliable. We use CV std as the explicit tie-breaker in winner selection.

6. When MAPE can mislead — and what to do about it

This section directly addresses your supervisor's likely follow-up: "can MAPE be untrue in cases?" The answer is yes, in five well-documented ways.

6.1 MAPE is asymmetric — it penalises over- and under-forecasts differently

Because the denominator is the actual value, a forecast that is 20 % too high gets a different MAPE than a forecast that is 20 % too low. Concretely: actual = 100, forecast = 120 → MAPE 20 %. Actual = 100, forecast = 80 → MAPE 20 %. Symmetric so far. But: actual = 100, forecast = 200 → MAPE 100 %; actual = 100, forecast = 0 → MAPE 100 %, capped — a forecast cannot be more than 100 % "below" actual. This biases minimisers toward under-forecasting.

6.2 MAPE explodes when the actual value is small or zero

If any test-set observation is near zero, MAPE blows up. Not an issue for our use case (annual TWh values > 40), but worth knowing if you ever forecast a quantity that can hit zero (e.g., monthly solar at high latitudes).

6.3 MAPE doesn't penalise bias

A model that is consistently 5 % too high every year and a model that alternates between +10 % and −0.5 % can have similar MAPE — but the first is biased and dangerous for capacity planning, the second is unbiased and just noisy. We do not currently report bias (mean error / MPE). Adding it would be a small improvement; see §8.

6.4 MAPE is not a statistical test — you cannot say "Model A is significantly better than Model B"

Prophet's CV mean MAPE (6.43 %) is lower than Holt-Winters' (9.07 %), but is that difference statistically meaningful? MAPE alone cannot tell you. The proper tool is the Diebold-Mariano test (Diebold & Mariano 1995), which compares two sequences of forecast errors and produces a p-value. We do not currently run it. This is a fair point your supervisor may raise — the honest reply is "agreed; adding the DM test is on the next-step list."

6.5 MAPE on a non-stationary series can flatter trend-following models

If demand is rising 4 %/yr, the denominator keeps growing. A flat-extrapolation model accrues % error slowly even when its absolute error is huge. RMSE catches this; that's why we report both.

Mitigations actually used in 03_forecasting.ipynb

  • Three metrics (MAPE, RMSE, R²) instead of relying on one.
  • Two evaluation schemes (single hold-out + 8-fold CV).
  • CV std reported alongside CV mean to expose lucky-on-average models.
  • Bootstrap CIs on the final winner's forecast, instead of pretending the point forecast is exact.

What a fuller version would add (honest forward-looking list)

  • MASE (Hyndman & Koehler 2006): MAE scaled by the in-sample naive-forecast MAE — symmetric, defined when actuals are zero, comparable across scales.
  • sMAPE: symmetric variant of MAPE bounded between 0 % and 200 %.
  • Mean Percentage Error (MPE) for bias detection.
  • Diebold-Mariano test for pairwise statistical comparison of forecast accuracy.
  • Quantile loss / pinball loss if probabilistic forecasts become a focus.

7. How the winner was selected (pre-registered rule)

  1. Restrict to models with CV mean MAPE < 10 % (Lewis "highly accurate").
  2. Among those, pick the one with the lowest CV std (most stable across folds).
  3. Tie-break with lowest hold-out MAPE.

After running the bench: six models pass step 1 — Prophet, Holt-Winters, SARIMAX-exog, ARIMA, RW+drift, OLS first-diff. Among those, Prophet has the lowest CV std (3.32 vs Holt-Winters 3.83). Winner: Prophet.

Defensive footnote: Holt-Winters has a lower hold-out MAPE (7.34 %) than Prophet (7.65 %). Why didn't it win? Because the rule prioritises stability across folds over single-test-set performance. A model that wins one hold-out by luck but is noisy across 8 folds is the wrong choice for a multi-year capacity-planning use case. Holt-Winters is reported as the strong runner-up in the dashboard.

8. Talking points for the defence

  1. "Why eleven models?" — Because no single algorithm is correct a priori. We benchmark across four families and let the data choose; the selection rule is pre-registered.
  2. "Why not XGBoost / LightGBM?" — The tree-ML family is in the bench as GradientBoosting. It scored CV MAPE 12.96 % and hold-out MAPE 19.5 %. Adding XGBoost would not change the categorical conclusion: tree models cannot extrapolate above the training-range maximum (mathematical limit, not tuning), and they overfit at n=24.
  3. "Why not LSTM / N-BEATS / Transformer?" — n = 24 annual observations. The Makridakis M3 and M4 competitions show statistical methods beat NN methods on short series. Neural nets need 100s to 1000s of obs to outperform.
  4. "Why didn't macro variables help?" — They did help at the margin (SARIMAX-exog CV 9.41 % vs ARIMA 9.68 %), but at n=24, ΔGDP variance is too small to identify a precise elasticity, and the elasticity estimate's standard error swamps the mean improvement. 03b_demand_drivers.ipynb diagnoses this and proposes the next-step fix (sectoral + climate disaggregation).
  5. "Why MAPE?" — Industry-standard, Lewis-calibrated, scale-free. We also report RMSE and R² to triangulate. We are aware of MAPE's asymmetry, zero-explosion, bias-blindness and lack of significance test (§6) — three of those (#1, #3, #5) are mitigated by reporting RMSE alongside.
  6. "What would you add next time?" — MASE, sMAPE, MPE for bias, Diebold-Mariano for pairwise testing, BSTS as an alternative to Prophet, and the driver/sectoral model in 03b_demand_drivers.ipynb.
  7. "Is the headline forecast trustworthy?" — At CV MAPE 6.4 %, yes as a trend extrapolation. It is blind to drivers (tariff, climate, industrial pipeline). The next layer (03b) is what makes the result robust to changes in drivers.
